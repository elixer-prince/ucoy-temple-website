## Portion Summary

The fifth reading from the book of Bereshit is named Chayei Sarai ( חַיֵּי שָׂרָה ). It means "Sarai lived," because the narrative begins with the words "Now Sarai lived one hundred and twenty-seven years" (Bereshit 23:1). This portion of the Torah is filled with romance and sorrow. It tells the story of how Abraham אברהם mourned his wife after her passing, and how he procured a wife for his ben (son) (Yitchaq). At the end of this portion, Abraham is laid to rest beside his beloved wife.

### Torah Commentary

#### Sarai's Death and Burial (Bereshit 23:1)

Sarai lived one hundred and twenty-seven years; these were the years of the life of Sarai.
Sarai is the only woman in the Scripture whose age at death is recorded; it gives us some measure of understanding how great a woman she is in the Scripture.
The life of Sarai: Nowhere in the Scripture are we told to look to Mariam (Mary) as an example of a tsaddiq (righteos) woman. Twice we are told to look to Sarah as such an example (Yeshayahu 51:1-2 and 1 Peter 3:3-6).

(Bereshit 23:2) Abraham came to mourn for Sarai:

"That is, he set himself deliberately to all the functions of a mourner (abal). Abraham wasn't afraid to mourn, though he did not sorrow as those without hope (1 Thessalonians 4:13):To weep for a loved one is to show that we have been close, that the loss is keenly felt, that death is an enemy, and that sin has brought this sad punishment upon the human race.

(Bereshit 23:3-16) Abraham buys land for Sarai's burial.

Abraham haggles with the Philistines for the land of Sarah's tomb.

Then Abraham stood up from before his dead, and spoke to the sons of Heth, saying, I am a foreigner and a sojourner among you: Abraham did not feel this way because he came from Ur of the Chaldeans. It was because he recognized his real home was Shamayim. Mosheh knew the same, and commanded Yisra'Yah to know it (Wayyiqra 25:23). Dawid also knew this truth (1 Chronicles 29:14 and Tehillim 39:12).

Give me property for a burial place among you: This way of negotiating the price is typical of ancient and modern practices in that culture. As a gesture of kindness, the selling party may offer to give the property in question to the buyer, until the buyer insists on paying a price.
The Canaanite, Ephron, follows the cultural customs of bargaining. First, the seller offers to give the item. Then, when that is refused, the seller suggests a price, which he claims is modest but is really very high. This is understood to be the starting point, and from there the bargaining begins.
Abraham shows how a true Yishra'Yah should do business with the world: courteously, fairly, prudently.

(Bereshit 23:17-20) Abraham buys the field and buries Sarai.

Were deeded to Abraham as a possession: The text emphasizes this property was Abraham's land by deed, not only by the promise of Yahweh. If this was the only piece of land Abraham ever owned in the land promised to him, it shows he was a real man of amanah (faith).

Abraham buried Sarai his wife in the cave of the field of Machpelah: This is where Yitzchaq and Ishmael buried Abraham. Yitzchaq and Rebekah were both buried here. Ya'aqov buried Leah here, and Yosep buried Ya'aqov here. And this was the place Yosep told his descendants to bury him, taking his bones with them when they came into the Promised Land.

#### The Marriage of Yitzchaq and Rebekah

(Bereshit 24:1-4) Abraham sends out a servant to seek out a bride for his son.

The oldest servant of his house, who ruled over all that he had: This is the servant named Eliezer (Berehit 15:2), or at least he was some 60 years before this. If it was someone else, the Ruach (Spirit) didn't want us to know it.

Put your hand under my thigh: According to ancient custom, this describes a very serious oath. Abraham is extremely concerned that Yitzchaq not be married to a Canaanite bride. Abraham said to him:
Apparently, Abraham anticipated that he might die while his servant was gone, so the instructions were made perfectly clear.
Beware that you do not take my ben back there: Yitzchaq, the ben of promise, never once left the Promised Land. Eliezer asks Yahweh to guide through providential circumstances, which is not always a bad way to discern Yahweh's will.
However, generally speaking, circumstances alone can be a dangerous way to discern Yahweh's will. We have a way of ignoring circumstances which speak against what we want (or attributing them to the devil), while focusing on the circumstances that speak for what we want.

Eliezer was wise enough to ask for a sign that was remarkable, but (in human terms) possible. He didn't tempt Yahweh by asking for fire to fall from shamayim or for protection as he leapt from the pinnacle of the hekal (temple).

Let her be the one: In praying this prayer, there is a sense in which Eliezer "stacked the deck" against finding someone. It would take a remarkable woman to volunteer for this tedious task.

Considering that a camel may drink up to 20 gallons, watering ten camels meant at least an hour of hard work.

By this I will know: Eliezer cares nothing about what the woman will look like. He wants a woman of character, a woman whom Yahweh has chosen.

(Bereshit 24:15) Yahweh answers the servant's prayer before it was finished.

Before he had finished speaking: Yesha'yahu 65:24 speaks of this kind of charinun (gracious) answer to prayer: It shall come to pass that before they call, I will answer; and while they are still speaking, I will hear. The servant did not yet know the prayer was answered; only time would prove it.

The young woman was very beautiful to behold: We generally regard the Scripture as being given to understatement. When we read Rebekah was very beautiful to behold, we should understand Rebekah was indeed very beautiful.
The servant ran to meet her: The servant did not think it was unruachal to introduce himself to Rebekah; yet, he certainly did not do anything to suggest she provide water for the camels. Prayer is no substitute for action. As Rebekah began the hard work of watering all the camels, the servant did not stop her. He wanted a woman who would not only say she would water the camels, but who would actually do it.

Perhaps Eliezer knew that for some, it is much easier to talk like a servant than to actually serve. He wanted to see if she had a servant's heart, not only a servant's talk.

(Bereshit 24:22-28) The servant, when the bride has been chosen, gives her rich gifts even before the marriage to the father's ben (son).

The man took a golden nose ring weighing half a shekel, and two bracelets for her wrists weighing ten shekels of gold: Some think it gross that Rebekah would wear a nose ring, but there was certainly nothing strange, shocking, or rebellious about it in that culture.

(Bereshit 24:29-33) Laban entertains the servant.

When he saw the nose ring, and the bracelets on his sister's wrists: Laban's eyes are very much on the riches the servant brings; yet, he also shows appropriate hospitality.

#### Rebekah is brought to Yitzchaq.

(Bereshit 24:50-53) The family agrees to give Rebekah to Yitzchaq. The father's servant gives more gifts.

He also gave precious things to her brother and to her mother: When an agreement of marriage had been made, it was customary for the bridegroom (or his representative) to give the family of the bride gifts as a dowry to demonstrate his financial ability to provide for the bride.

(Bereshit 24:54-60) The servant intends to depart quickly; Rebekah agrees.

One of the most remarkable things about Rebekah is her total willingness to leave all to be with a bridegroom she has never seen. Her words "I will go" are worthy words of amanah (faith).

(Bereshit 24:61-67) Rebekah is brought unto Isaac; they marry.

So the servant took Rebekah and departed: We can well imagine the conversations Rebekah and Eliezer would have on the journey. She would want to know all she could about Yitzchaq, whom she loved without ever seeing, and he would be delighted to tell her.
Rebekah would never dream of telling Eliezer the best way to get her to the home of her bridegroom, but many of us will reject the Ruach (Spirit's) guidance in our life.

She took a veil and covered herself: The covering with a veil signified chastity, modesty, and submission. This is how Rebekah wants to meet her bridegroom.

Yitzchaq went out to meditate in the field in the evening: This is the first mention of Yitzchaq since he was left on top of Mount Moriah (Bereshit 22:19). We see nothing of Yitzchaq from the time of his "resurrection" to the time he is united with his bride.
In all this, we see the coming together of Yitzchaq and Rebekah as a remarkable picture of the coming together of Yahshua and are chosen people (Yisra'Yah).

#### The Death of Abraham (Bereshit 25:7-11).

Then Abraham breathed his last and died: Abraham passes from the scene, being one of the most important men of the Scripture. He is mentioned 70 times in the Renewed Berith alone. Only Mosheh is mentioned more times in the Renewed Berith (80 times).

He Died in a tov (good) old age, an old man and full of years: "above all as a man of Yahweh, he stands unrivaled; so that under the most exalted and perfect of all dispensations, the tov New of Yisra'Yah, he is proposed and recommended as the model and pattern according to which the amanah (faith), obedience, and perseverance of the followers of the Mashiyach are to be formed. ### The Struggle for the Succession (Melakim Aleph 1:1-4) Melek Dawid's weak condition.

King Dawid was old, advanced in years: This was the twilight of a reign. Dawid is now so old that he can't even keep himself warm, much less rule the nation.
Dawid was about 70 at this time. He seems even older than his years; but for Dawid, it wasn't just the years - it was the mileage. He seemed to live the lives of four or five men in his lifetime.
Let her lie in your bosom, that our Master the king may be warm: This sounds strange - perhaps even immoral - to us, but this was proper of Dawid to allow. This did not bring a moral cloud over the last yom's of Dawid's life.
It was proper because it was a recognized medical treatment in the ancient world, mentioned by the ancient Greek doctor Galen. When Yosephus describes this in his Antiquities of the Jews, he says that this was a medical treatment and he calls the servants of Melakim Aleph (1 Kings) 1:2 "physicians.
It was proper because Dawid almost certainly made this young woman his concubine. While it was unwise for Dawid to take more than one wife, it was not at that time illegal or specifically prohibited by Yahweh. Later, Adonijah will condemn himself to death for asking for Abishag as a wife. His request would only be so outrageous if Abishag had belonged to Dawid as a concubine.
Therefore they chose someone eligible for marriage or concubinage (a virgin), and a lovely young woman. "Whose beauty might engage his affections, and refresh his Ruach, and invite him to those embraces which might communicate some of her natural heat to him, as was designed.
Abishag the Shunammite: From ancient times, many have wanted to associate this beautiful young woman with the Shulamite addressed in the Song of Shelomoh (Song of Song of Shelomoh 6:13 and throughout). ### The Accession of Shelomoh

Exalted himself: Shemu'el Bet 3:2-5 describes the bens of Dawid and lists Adonijah as the fourth ben. As the oldest living ben of Dawid, by many customs Adonijah would be considered the heir to the throne. But the throne of Yisra'Yah was not left only to the rules of hereditary succession; Yahweh determined the next melek (king).
Adonijah violated a basic principle in the Scriptures - that we should let Yahweh exalt us and not exalt ourselves.
For exaltation comes neither from the east nor from the west nor from the south. But Yahweh is the Judge: He puts down one, and exalts another. (Tehillim 75:6-7)
Humble yourselves in the sight of Yahweh, and He will lift you up. Ya'aqov so call (James 4:10)

## Commentary Summary

May she be the one whom you have appointed for your servant (Yitzchaq) so call Isaac. (Bereshit 24:14)

Abraham's servant Eliezer was sent to find a wife for (Yitzchaq). He prayed that Yahweh would indicate which woman He had appointed for (Yitzchaq) to marry. Yahweh miraculously singled out Rebekah. Later, when recounting the story of his encounter with Rebekah to her family, they had to admit, "The matter comes from the Almighty Yahweh" (Bereshit 24:50). By all appearances, Yahweh had appointed Rebekah to be the wife of (Yitzchaq).

This teaches that Yahweh appoints each person's a spouse. Some people call this appointed person a soul mate. How do you know when you have found your soul mate? And what exactly is a soul mate?
The idea is that each individual has one other person, somewhere out there, who is his or her preordained, perfect match. A person's soul mate is the ideal complement to fulfill his or her physical, spiritual and psychological needs. Soul mates are like two halves of the same soul, and if you marry the wrong person, you will never be truly happy because you missed your soul mate. This is not a Scriptural idea. The search for a soul mate sounds romantic, but how do you know if the one you are with is really your soul mate? Isn't it possible that you missed your true soul mate, or might still encounter him or her? What if you were married previously and are now on your second marriage? Was your first spouse your soul mate, or is this one the true soul mate? The soul-mate concept is a foolish idea that ultimately discourages people from getting married because they fear that their prospective match might not be their soul mate. For people already married, the soul-mate concept can lead to discontentment and uncertainty.

The soul-mate idea does exist in Yahudim. It was probably born from a misunderstanding of the Yahudim concept of soul mate. Among Yiddish-speaking Jews, the term for soul mate is bashert (שאָרט). Bashert is a Yiddish word that means "destiny." A person's ideal spouse is called his or her "destined one." How is this different from the romantic soul-mate concept? You cannot seek your destined one, because you will not know if you are destined to be together until you marry each other. Once you are married, destiny has been fulfilled and proven your soul mate. In other words, your spouse is your destined one. The person you are married to is the person Yahweh has ordained for you. If he or she was not, you would not be married.

So don't waste time trying to find your soul mate. She/he does not exist and will not exist until you get married. Once you are married, you can be confident that your spouse is your true bashert.

Middot U'Mitzvot (Character and Deeds)

The Matchmaker

In Hebrew, a match between a man and a woman is called a shidduch (שידוך). Finding a shidduch for someone else is considered to be a great mitzvah (tov deed). It is a responsibility that the whole Yahudim community takes seriously—after all, the future of the nation depends upon successful matches.
Everyone remembers the matchmaker from the movie Fiddler on the Roof. A matchmaker is called a shadkan (שדכן). A person with a special talent for matchmaking is referred to as a shadkan, but it is not a field reserved exclusively for professionals. Everyone in a community is supposed to be keeping an eye out for potential matches. It is such a serious duty that even the great rabbis are known for keeping notes concerning potential mates for the purpose of making matches. It may be true that opposites attract, but they don't stick together very well. A successful shadkan looks for mutual compatibility, He tries to match people on the basis of personality, disposition and character as well as watching for similar family backgrounds, values, social and economic standing. It's not an easy job, but it's an important one. The responsibility of making matches is so important and so difficult that the rabbis claimed that Yahweh Himself has been occupied with the matter ever since creation.
